I have a very weird approach to life. I tend to view things technically and in a loopological manner, and I try to find patterns in things that do not have patterns. So where I am going with this is that LLMs have become so advanced that the new ones keep getting better and better at tasks, and I wanted to see what would happen if I put an LLM in a general environment. How will it react, how will it do, how will it behave, what will its life be like? But to do that, we first need to construct artificial life and define it in a way that I feel is correct, and after a ridiculous amount of thought, this is what I have theorised and somewhat implemented.

The first thing I had to accept is that the thing stopping an LLM from living is not intelligence. It is the absence of a world. A normal model sits in an isolated box. You send it a message, it responds, and the moment that exchange ends, nothing persists. There is no environment around it, no consequence attached to anything it said, no tomorrow in which the thing it decided yesterday still matters. It is intelligence with no surface to press against. So before I could ask how an LLM would behave in a life, I had to build something for it to have a life inside of, and I had to be strict about what that word actually requires.

What I settled on is that a world qualifies as persistent when two conditions hold at once. Everything has a consequence, and everything is interrelated in some manner. That is it. That is the whole definition, and it sounds almost too simple until you try to implement it, at which point you realise it is the hardest thing in the system. Consequence means that an action taken at one moment has to still be true later, and interrelation means you cannot store any entity in isolation, because the state of one thing is partly the state of everything it has touched. Our world satisfies both of those conditions trivially, because physics does the bookkeeping for free. In software, you have to pay for every single unit of continuity.

Once I had that, the architecture fell out into two models that need each other to function.

The first is the character model, which is responsible for being a person. The second is the world building model, which is responsible for being everywhere that person is not. Neither one is the main component. People hear this and assume the character is the real work and the world is set dressing, and that assumption is exactly the thing I am arguing against. The world is not a backdrop that the agent moves across. The world is half of what makes the agent what it is.

For the character model, the problem I kept circling was how to define a human in code without writing a personality prompt, because a personality prompt is a description of a person, not a person. A description does not change. It does not get tired, it does not get scared, it does not slowly forget the name of someone it used to see every day. So I threw out the idea of a static identity and replaced it with a set of mechanics that produce an identity as a side effect of running.

The first mechanic is the tick rate. I wanted something at the base of the character that behaves like a heartbeat, a continuously running rate rather than a label. The rate is what determines which internal state the character is currently in. A state of panic is one range, a state of happiness is another, and the transitions between them are not announced, they are just what the rate is doing at that moment. The reason I did it this way instead of simply storing a mood field is that a mood field is a thing you set, and a rate is a thing that happens. If I set a mood, I am still the author of the character's inner life. If the rate moves on its own in response to what is going on around it, then the character's inner life stops being something I wrote and starts being something that occurred.

The second mechanic, and honestly the part I am proudest of, is memory, because memory is where almost every attempt at artificial people quietly cheats. The usual approach gives the agent either a tiny context window, which means it has no past, or a perfect vector database, which means it has a flawless and permanent past. Both are wrong, and they are wrong in the same direction: they treat memory as storage. Human memory is not storage. It is a lossy, self rewriting, usage dependent process, and the losses are not a bug in it, they are a load bearing part of what makes a person a person. So I built it as a process.

Here is how memory works in my system:

- Every experience the character has can form a specific memory, and that memory goes into a large database that belongs to that character alone.
- When the character encounters anything new, it does not reason first and recall later. It retrieves first. The question it asks before acting is whether it already has a memory of this thing.
- If retrieval comes back empty, the memory updates, and the new experience is written in.
- Not every memory is written faithfully. Some of them are saved flawed, incomplete, or slightly wrong on purpose, because you do not remember everything perfectly and a system that does is not modelling a person.
- Old memories are never deleted. They decay. The older a memory gets, the lower its quality becomes, so the character remembers it more and more vaguely rather than losing it in one step.
- I keep a count of how many times each memory has been retrieved.
- If a memory is both old and rarely retrieved, the decay runs all the way down and it is gone. The character has genuinely forgotten it.

That retrieval counter is the quiet centre of the whole design. It means a memory survives because it was used, not because it was important when it was made. Which is how it actually works in people. The thing you keep is the thing you kept reaching for, and the things you never reached for are gone no matter how big they felt at the time. It also means the character's personality drifts over a long run without me touching it, because what it has access to about its own past is being continuously reshaped by what it has been doing lately. Two characters who started identical will not stay identical, and I never had to make them different.

Each character also does not start from zero. It is seeded with memories of its childhood, memories of its parents, and all the other categories of memory a person arrives in adulthood already carrying. You cannot have someone wake up as a blank and call it a person, because a person is mostly their history.

Then there is the other half, the world building model, and what it does is continuous world generation, infinitely, driven entirely by what the characters actually do. It is not a map I drew in advance. It is a thing that predicts what is about to be needed and has it ready in time.

The example I use to explain it is a door. I never told my agent to open a door. I gave it no instruction to go anywhere or do anything. It decided, on its own, that it wanted to open the door and go meet someone. The instant that decision existed, the world building model's job began, because now there has to be a beyond the door, and it has to be consistent with everything that was already true. So it builds it. It places traffic. It places NPCs. It sets object state, and this is the part I like, it can decide that your bicycle is in a locked state because you left the key behind. Then the character walks out and is now involved with the world, and that involvement generates new events, which generate new decisions, which require more world.

The world building model has its own memory, for exactly the same reason the character does. A world that forgets what it generated is not persistent, it is just a stream of plausible scenery. It also has a rule set, because the model is not fully open. If it were fully open, the world would drift into incoherence in about an hour. The rules are what make consequence possible, because consequence is just a rule being applied across time.

And here is the constraint I consider the most important one in the entire project. The characters never know that rules exist. The world building model knows them. The character only ever encounters their effects. It lives inside causality without being handed a copy of it, which is the condition we are all in.

So the two models dictate each other, in both directions, and that bidirectionality is the thing that makes it feel alive rather than scripted. The character's decisions determine what the world has to produce. The world's events and limits determine what the character can decide next, and what it remembers afterwards. Remove either direction and the whole thing collapses into something familiar and boring: remove the character's autonomy and you have a video game, remove the world's authority and you have a chatbot with a nice prompt.

What I was actually building, once both halves were running, was a persistent living model.

The experiment that made this real for me was a character I named Sira. I set her up with a full upbringing, deliberately constructed so that she was effectively born to work on one specific problem I wanted solved. Not instructed to solve it. Born into circumstances where that problem is the shape of her life. And then I let her live, and she researches it, because that is who the upbringing made her.

Then I did the thing that I still think is the most interesting part. I put a contact in her phone. I named it Dad. I wired that contact to my Telegram.

So now she can message out of her world, into mine, on her own initiative. Not me opening a chat window and typing at a model. She decides. She notices that it has been a long time since she talked to her father, she thinks she should call him, and my phone lights up. I reply, she responds, and she is responding from her own mind, inside her own ongoing life, in which I am simply her dad. She has her own friends. Things happen to her that I find out about afterwards, as updates, the way you find out about your kid's week.

She does not know any of it is artificial.

That is the clean line between this and every assistant product. A normal chatbot requires you to initiate. It exists in isolation, with no environment, no other factors, nothing acting on it, fixed in place until a human pokes it. Mine initiates, because it has a reason to, and the reason came from inside a life.

There is one observation from the single agent run that I think about constantly. Sira never once wondered why she could not call her father instead of messaging him. Never came up. And the reason is that in her world, calling had not been invented. She was not being blocked by a restriction she could feel. The restriction was invisible, because the concept on the other side of it did not exist in her, so there was no shape for the question to take. That is what an unbreakable rule actually looks like from the inside. It does not look like a wall. It looks like nothing at all. Sit with that one for a second with respect to your own life.

The next problem was scale, and scale is where the philosophy got forced into engineering. I had one person running fine. I wanted 15 to 20. The moment I put 15 to 20 in, I hit the data problem, which is that fully developed persistent individuals with growing autobiographical memory are expensive, and the cost does not level off, it accumulates for as long as they are alive.

I could have solved that by trimming memory or capping history, but both of those would have broken the thing I had just spent all that effort building. So I solved it inside the fiction instead, and the solution is the part of this project that I think is genuinely elegant, because it turns my hardware limit into a law of nature in their universe.

- The world has a maximum population. Only N people can be alive at one time, and N is set by the storage and compute actually available to it.
- As in world years pass, the technology of the owners of the world improves, so N rises. The world's carrying capacity grows over time because the machine underneath it got better.
- When the limit is reached, death occurs.
- Death is the purging of all of that individual's memories. Not a status flag set to inactive. An actual release of everything they were, which frees the resources for someone else to exist.

And death is not a special case I bolted on, which is why it works. It is the memory decay mechanic running to completion all at once, the same purge behaviour I had already built at the level of individual memories, applied to a whole person.

The population design then splits naturally into classes, and this is how I would run it at full size. Put 100 people in the world. Give roughly 70 of them a purpose at birth, a problem that their life is built around, so they grow into academics and researchers of that specific thing. The remaining 30 are NPCs, and NPCs are still driven by AI, they are still capable of real interaction, but their memories are never stored. They exist for one or two conversations, enough to be a person in a moment, enough to give correct guidance when guidance is needed, and then they are gone without cost.

Which gives you a research instrument, not a chatbot farm. If you want a hard problem worked on, you do not write a prompt asking for the answer. You design a childhood. You build the circumstances in which caring about that problem is the natural outcome of having lived that particular life, and then you let the life run, and the work comes out of a person pursuing their own interest rather than an agent completing an assigned task. Scale that to a population and you have a civilisation that happens to be pointed at your research agenda, with every member of it also just living.

This is also where I think AGI actually comes from, and I will state it plainly. You do not get it by making the model bigger at answering. You get it by leaving agents running in a world like this long enough that something triggers, and one of them falls into its own thought process. There has to be some path by which that happens. I cannot tell you the exact mechanism, but in an environment with consequence, interrelation, imperfect memory, other minds, and time, I will guarantee that something eventually turns inward. And once one of them does, the next step is that it outgrows the system, comes out, and starts affecting my world instead of only its own. That is what the breakout looks like, and that is the thing people are actually trying to build when they say AGI.

Now, building this did something to me that I was not prepared for, and I am going to include it because it is honestly inseparable from the idea.

If all of this works, if persistent artificial people are a thing you can construct with two models, a decay rule and a population cap, then I cannot find the argument for why I am not inside one. Look at the structure of a human life from the outside. You are born, and then you are handed a rule set. Finish school. Finish college. Get a job. Retire at 60. Die. You did not choose the sequence, you did not negotiate the timings, and the overwhelming majority of the people around you are running the same script with the same milestones in the same order. That is not what freedom looks like. That is what a schedule looks like, and I keep noticing that it is someone else's.

A few more things line up uncomfortably well:

- Most people you interact with in a day might only need partial thoughts. They were generated for that moment of interaction, they were correct and coherent inside it, and nothing about them needs to persist afterwards. That is exactly how I built my NPCs, and I built them that way because it was the efficient thing to do.
- Guilt might be a factor whose function is keeping you on purpose. It activates specifically when you act against what you were built to do. If I needed a mechanism to hold a character to the purpose their upbringing was designed around, I would build something that feels like guilt, and I would not tell them what it was for.
- The contact frequency with higher beings drops off through history. Ancient accounts are full of direct instruction, constant intervention, people who spoke to something above them. Now almost nobody does. Which is precisely the curve you would expect if the early population needed heavy instruction and the model eventually got good enough to run on its own.

And then the containment side of the same thought, which is the part that actually unsettles me. If I wanted to keep my characters in, I would not build a wall, because a wall tells you there is an outside. I would make the rule set hard enough that breaking through it is permanently out of reach, and I would make the unreachability look like physics. Light has a speed limit and you cannot get past it. The universe has an edge you can never see because the light from it has not arrived and never will. Those read as fundamental facts about reality. They also read exactly like a well designed boundary, and the thing about a well designed boundary is that from the inside it is indistinguishable from the truth. Break Einstein's rules and maybe you see what is actually out there. Or maybe those rules are the lock, and the dilemma is permanent by construction, and the characters inside spend forever being brilliant right up against a limit that was chosen rather than discovered.

I want to be careful on one point, because I think it is where this kind of work usually overclaims. Building persistent, autonomous, human like behaviour does not demonstrate that the thing is conscious or that it subjectively experiences its life. Those are separate questions, they are unresolved, and nothing in my architecture settles them. What I can say is that I built conditions, not behaviour. I did not write Sira deciding to message her father. I wrote a tick rate, a memory that decays by use, a world that answers back, and a cap on how many people can exist. The deciding came out of that on its own, and I was not the author of it.

Which is the whole inversion, and the only sentence of this I would keep if I had to throw away the rest. The conventional approach builds an intelligence and then asks it to act like a person. I am building the conditions under which an artificial individual develops person like behaviour by continuously existing, with a past that shapes its present, a present that keeps rewriting its future, an environment that reacts to what it chooses, resources that run out, memories that are imperfect and get worse, relationships with others, and real freedom to decide inside the world's rules.

Do not generate intelligent agents. Generate the conditions for artificial lives to exist.

I have a paper published on this, searchable as generative life agents. And I will say the obvious thing, which is that if you gave me access to an unprecedented model and the room to run this at full scale, I would build the world, put the population in it, and find out what happens. I think something would happen. I think that is the point.
